Some time ago, I visited a sewage treatment plant in Apeldoorn, in the Netherlands. I arrived expecting a lesson in waste disposal. I left thinking about energy, agriculture, public health and the opportunities we keep washing away in our own cities.

What stayed with me was not simply the machinery. It was the thinking behind the system: collect wastewater properly, treat it carefully, recover what is useful and take responsibility for what remains.

Standing there, my thoughts returned to Nairobi. To Ruai. To our rivers, our growing estates and the farming communities downstream. Why should something that can yield useful water, energy and nutrients remain such a persistent burden on our neighbourhoods?

That is the gold I see in Nairobi's sewage. Not effortless wealth, and certainly not a reason to romanticise pollution. It is the value we could recover by doing an essential public service properly.

First, collect the sewage. Then talk about the gold.

A treatment plant cannot clean wastewater that never reaches it. The lesson begins before the first tank: at the household connection, the business premises, the sewer, the pump and the point where a blockage is reported and repaired.

Apeldoorn's water authority, Waterschap Vallei en Veluwe, describes a connected sequence. Screens remove coarse material; settling separates solids; biological treatment reduces pollution; further settling separates water from the active sludge. The treated water is checked before discharge to a receiving water body.

For Nairobi, I would start with an unglamorous question: where does every wastewater stream actually go? A sewer drawn on a plan is not the same as a functioning connection. A treatment facility's nameplate capacity is not proof that the incoming load is being treated to the required standard.

Nor should sanitation stop at the edge of the sewer network. Households using septic tanks and pit latrines also need safe emptying, transport and treatment. The World Health Organization's sanitation safety planning approach considers risks across the service chain, not just inside the plant. That is the standard of thinking I want our counties to adopt.

Nature does important work. Engineering makes it dependable.

One of the most striking things about the visit was the role of biology. In aerated treatment, bacteria break down organic pollution with the help of oxygen. The water authority explains how aerators supply that oxygen and keep the contents moving, after which the bacterial sludge is separated and much of it returned to the process. Nature is doing useful work, but inside a deliberately managed system.

Calling the process biological does not mean that sewage becomes safe by being left alone. Pumps, controls, maintenance, sampling and competent operators are part of the treatment. The right combination also depends on what is entering the plant and what quality the outgoing water must achieve.

For African cities, the objective should not be to copy an impressive foreign installation equipment for equipment. It should be to choose a treatment process we can operate, maintain and monitor reliably under local conditions.

From sludge to useful energy

The solids removed during treatment still contain organic material. Through anaerobic digestion, microorganisms break down suitable material without oxygen and produce biogas, consisting mainly of methane and carbon dioxide. This is a different biological stage from the oxygen-fed treatment of the wastewater.

At Apeldoorn, that gas is used to generate electricity and heat. Equipment supplier 2G describes a gas-engine combined heat-and-power installation at the works. The practical point is not that every sewage plant needs turbines. It is that properly managed sludge can help meet useful energy demand.

The water authority's published 2024 figures make the opportunity tangible: Apeldoorn generated 11,650 megawatt-hours of electricity and supplied 4,750 megawatt-hours to the electricity grid. It also supplied surplus heat to the nearby Zuidbroek district heating network. These are reported results from that plant, not a forecast for Nairobi.

For a Kenyan utility, I would first examine how much purchased electricity a dependable energy-recovery system could replace. Selling surplus power is a separate commercial and regulatory question; a project should not assume a buyer, a tariff or a grid connection before those arrangements exist.

Neither should it count every cubic metre of gas as a benefit while ignoring leakage, safety and equipment downtime. Energy recovery must strengthen the sanitation service, not distract from it.

The nutrients we should not simply throw away

Energy is only part of the story. Apeldoorn also recovers struvite, a material used in fertiliser production. The water authority reports that its Apeldoorn and Amersfoort facilities together produced 445 tonnes of struvite in 2024, which was sold as an intermediate product to the fertiliser industry. That combined figure should not be attributed to Apeldoorn alone.

The chemistry matters. Conventional struvite is magnesium ammonium phosphate: it contains magnesium, nitrogen and phosphorus, rather than being a ready-made nitrogen-phosphorus-potassium blend. VITO's technical explanation describes a controlled recovery process in which a magnesium source can be added to precipitate dissolved phosphate and ammonium. Potassium recovery is a different question, not an automatic output of this process.

That distinction does not weaken the opportunity. It makes it more credible. A recovered nutrient product should have a known composition, suitable quality controls, regulatory acceptance and a customer who can use it.

As someone interested in agribusiness, I see a useful connection here between the city and the farm. But farmers should receive a tested, correctly specified input, not a promise that anything coming out of a treatment plant is good for the soil.

The remaining solids still need a responsible destination

Recovering gas does not make every remaining solid harmless. The US Environmental Protection Agency explains that digestion leaves a residual material, often called digestate, whose further treatment and use depend on the material and intended application. Its guidance distinguishes the biogas from the residual solids and liquids; they are not interchangeable products.

Vallei en Veluwe's own process description identifies an energy-recovery route in which digested sludge is sent for use as fuel at a power station. That does not establish the destination of every batch, or make every sludge suitable for combustion or agricultural use. The appropriate route must be established for the actual material.

My rule would be simple: no residue should become someone else's unrecorded problem. A recovery project should be able to show what leaves the site, how it was tested, who receives it and what happens next.

Could treated Nairobi wastewater support farming in Ukambani?

This is an idea worth examining seriously. Could properly treated wastewater support suitable irrigation schemes in parts of Ukambani, rather than carrying pollution downstream? I believe the potential deserves a feasibility study, not a casual promise that a pipe from Nairobi will solve agricultural water scarcity.

Water suitable for discharge is not automatically drinking water, and water that looks clear is not proof of safety. The WHO guidelines on wastewater use in agriculture consider risks to farm workers, nearby communities and consumers. Appropriate treatment, exposure controls and monitoring must match the proposed use.

A Kenyan irrigation proposal would need to establish dependable flows, treatment performance, storage, conveyance costs, crop suitability, soil effects and the ability to pay for operation. Microbiological hazards, salinity and relevant chemical contaminants cannot be omitted from that assessment. Kenya's 2024 Water Quality Regulations distinguish standards for different uses and for discharge to sewers and the environment.

I would begin with a manageable scheme and identified users, close enough to a dependable source to make the economics testable. Expansion should follow measured performance. Downstream communities must help shape the project; they should not merely be presented with another upstream decision.

The ambition is worthwhile: more reliable water for appropriate uses, less pressure on freshwater supplies and rivers that are no longer treated as drains. But safety has to be designed into the project from the beginning.

Ruai deserves evidence, protection and a voice

Ruai should be central to this conversation, not a place mentioned only when Nairobi needs somewhere to send its wastewater. Residents around treatment facilities deserve reliable services, responsive management and a meaningful say in decisions affecting their neighbourhoods.

I would not declare every Ruai borehole unsafe, or attribute contamination in a particular borehole to heavy metals, without representative sampling and laboratory evidence. The opposite claim - that groundwater is safe because it comes from a borehole - is not a substitute for testing either. WHO's risk-based guidance makes assessment and monitoring fundamental to protection.

My demand would be practical: publish water-quality results in language residents can understand, identify and control pollution sources, investigate complaints, and provide a clear response when a risk is found. Groundwater investigations must examine the relevant sources and pathways rather than assume one explanation for an entire area.

Odour complaints also deserve action. I would want properly maintained processes, a documented odour-control plan and appropriate monitoring of worker and community exposure. A strong smell is not a laboratory measurement of a specific toxic pollutant, but dismissing residents' experience is not an acceptable management strategy.

Resource recovery will never earn public trust if nearby households are asked to tolerate poor conditions for the sake of an electricity or fertiliser headline.

Industrial pollution must be controlled before it enters the system

Municipal treatment should not become an excuse for uncontrolled industrial discharges. NEMA's explanation of the Water Quality Regulations sets out responsibilities for discharges into sewer systems and requires an effluent discharge licence for releases into the environment. Treatment is not a licence to pollute upstream.

The Nairobi Rivers Commission identifies industrial effluent, untreated domestic sewage and solid-waste dumping among the basin's pollution pressures. That is why a river-restoration programme must do more than remove visible rubbish from the banks.

I would make industrial discharge control, source identification and enforcement part of every recovery programme. The goal is to protect the treatment process and the people using the river, while giving recovered products a chance of meeting credible quality standards.

A cleaner river cannot depend entirely on a treatment operator trying to undo whatever everyone else releases.

Africa is already building parts of this future

We do not need to treat resource recovery as an exclusively European idea. The World Bank's wastewater-to-resource work includes Durban's use of treated wastewater for industrial customers. Its wider case-study programme also documents approaches in Dakar and New Cairo. These are different projects with different circumstances, not a single model to copy unchanged.

Closer to home, PIDG reported the groundbreaking for an expansion of Sanivation's Naivasha treatment operation in September 2026. The model uses treated faecal sludge and waste from nearby sawmills to produce fuel briquettes. The expansion is a construction project, not evidence that all its future capacity is already operating.

These examples encourage me because they connect sanitation with a real use for the recovered output. One location may have an industrial water buyer. Another may have a viable solid-fuel market. A larger works may have suitable conditions for biogas or nutrient recovery.

The best project is not necessarily the one that promises every possible product. It is the one that delivers safe treatment and has a defensible market for what it chooses to recover.

Build the operating institution, not just the plant

Kenya is not starting with a blank sheet. In February 2026, the Government Advertising Agency published an Athi Water Works Development Agency invitation for bids for Phase II of the Dandora wastewater treatment plant expansion. An invitation to tender is an important step, but it is not proof of completed construction or compliant daily operation.

I would judge investment by what happens after commissioning: actual flows treated, effluent quality, equipment availability, response to failures and the experience of nearby residents. Those measures should remain visible long after the launch photographs have been taken.

Even Apeldoorn offers a warning against assuming that good infrastructure cannot fail. The water authority reports a June 2026 power interruption that allowed untreated wastewater into the Apeldoorn Canal, followed by containment and clean-up measures. That makes dependable power, alarms, contingency capacity and transparent incident reporting part of the lesson too.

For our counties, I would insist on three things. First, protect the operating budget: staff, electricity, laboratory work, spare parts and preventive maintenance cannot be optional. Second, make performance public and independently verifiable. Third, connect each investment to a whole sanitation plan, including affordable services for households outside the sewer network.

Public investment, development finance and private participation can all be considered. But the contract should reward sustained service and environmental performance, not just delivery of concrete and equipment.

Recover value without promising that sewage pays for everything

There is a business case to investigate here, but it needs discipline. The World Bank describes how recovery and reuse can improve the economics of sanitation. That is not a guarantee that electricity, fertiliser or reclaimed-water sales will cover every plant's costs.

I would separate income from savings. Electricity used on site may reduce a bill; it is not necessarily cash received. Recovered material may have a theoretical value; it is not revenue until a qualified buyer pays. A carbon-credit forecast should not be the money on which essential maintenance depends.

The financial assessment should include collection, treatment, product testing, storage, transport, residual disposal and the cost of keeping the service reliable. It should also recognise the public purpose. We should not require a river to produce a commercial return before we decide that people deserve protection from untreated sewage.

This is where I see space for social entrepreneurship: safe collection and emptying services, equipment maintenance, testing, resource processing and dependable market connections. Enterprise should help deliver the public service, not leave low-income households outside it.

The real gold is a healthier city

My visit to Apeldoorn changed the way I look at what leaves our homes and businesses. Wastewater is not simply a substance to move out of sight. It is a responsibility - and, when managed well, an opportunity.

Nairobi and other African cities should pursue that opportunity with ambition and honesty. Recover energy where the conditions support it. Recover nutrients that meet the right standards. Reuse water where treatment, monitoring and economics make it appropriate. Manage the remaining solids responsibly.

The gold in Nairobi's sewage is not only what we can sell. It is the cleaner river, the protected neighbourhood, the useful water and the public trust we can recover.

Let us clean our counties. Let us keep raw sewage out of our rivers and streets. And let us build sanitation systems that continue working after the speeches are over.
