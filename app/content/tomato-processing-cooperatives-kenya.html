My earlier note on tomato farming was deliberately provocative. It argued that when many farmers plant the same perishable crop at the same time, blaming government for every price collapse misses part of the problem. I still agree with the central point—but I would make the argument more carefully today.

Farmers do not control the entire market system. Roads, market information, standards, finance, irrigation, research, competition policy and public infrastructure all matter. But Kenya’s tomato value chain also has a clear enterprise gap: too much value is still lost between fresh production and processing.

The processing gap is measurable

KALRO’s 2024 Annual Report, published in 2026, states that less than 2% of locally produced fresh tomatoes go into processing. It estimates about 93 tomato processors in Kenya and notes that processors still import tomato puree from Egypt. KALRO identifies processing and value addition as important for reducing post-harvest losses, extending shelf life, improving distribution and increasing profitability and export potential.

That is a remarkable mismatch. Farmers can experience gluts and low farm-gate prices while processors still depend partly on imported intermediate products.

Oversupply and shortage can exist at the same time

This sounds contradictory until we separate “fresh tomato supply” from “processing-grade supply delivered to a processor under predictable conditions.”

A factory needs the right varieties, volumes, quality, delivery schedule and price. A farmer may produce excellent tomatoes for the fresh market that are not suitable for industrial paste. A processor cannot build a reliable business around random surpluses that appear only after prices collapse.

That means the solution is not simply “build a factory.” It is coordinated production.

Cooperatives can be part of the answer

I still believe farmer-owned or farmer-linked processing deserves more attention. A cooperative or producer company can aggregate volume, organise planting calendars, negotiate inputs, standardise varieties and invest jointly in grading, drying, pulping, cold storage or small-scale processing.

But cooperative ownership is not automatically efficient. Governance, professional management, working capital and market discipline matter. A badly governed cooperative can destroy value just as easily as a badly run private company.

The question should therefore be: what ownership and operating model gives farmers bargaining power while keeping the processing business commercially competent?

Micro-processing should start with a market

Before buying equipment, a group should know who will buy the output. Tomato paste, puree, sauce, dried tomato, powder and institutional cooking products each have different standards, packaging needs and margins.

A sensible sequence is:

  • identify a buyer or underserved market;
  • work backwards to the required product specification;
  • select the right processing varieties;
  • coordinate production volumes and planting dates;
  • model raw-material and energy costs;
  • pilot the processing line;
  • then expand only after the buyer and economics are proven.

Government has a role—but not every role

My old note said government exists to regulate, not to run every business. I would now put it differently. Government has legitimate roles in research, extension, food safety, roads, irrigation, market information, standards, finance frameworks and competition. Those are public functions that can make private enterprise possible.

What government cannot sustainably do is guarantee a profitable market for every crop planted without coordination. Price risk is part of agriculture. The better response is to build institutions and businesses that reduce avoidable risk.

Land use makes the question more urgent

As productive land around towns is converted to housing and commercial development, the remaining agricultural land has to create more value per hectare. That makes post-harvest handling and processing increasingly important. Producing more tonnes without improving storage, logistics and markets can simply produce more waste.

My view today

The opportunity in tomatoes is not only farming. It is the system around farming: nurseries, irrigation, extension, market data, aggregation, cold chain, processing, packaging and distribution.

For social entrepreneurs, that is especially interesting because the commercial and social problems overlap. A viable processor can reduce losses, create local jobs, give farmers more predictable demand and substitute some imports. But it has to be designed as a real enterprise, not as a rescue project that only operates during a glut.

The most useful question for farmers is therefore not “Who will buy what we have already planted?” It is “What does the market need, and how do we organise production and processing around that demand?”
