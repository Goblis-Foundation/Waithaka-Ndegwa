Social entrepreneurship has spent years improving the language of impact: outcomes, indicators, theories of change, additionality and evidence. That work matters. But a harder question is becoming more important: who will actually pay for the outcomes we say we create?

Several developments around the first week of November 2026 point in the same direction. On 4 November, Social Finance announced two new impact-first investments designed to reach markets and people that conventional capital often overlooks. On 5–6 November, social-innovation practitioners, funders, researchers and policymakers are gathering in Nairobi for the Wayfinder convening. And a recent World Economic Forum and Schwab Foundation paper argues that better impact disclosure is not enough unless it becomes connected to real economic demand.

For African social entrepreneurs, this is more than a debate about terminology. It goes to the centre of how a mission-driven enterprise survives, grows and remains accountable.

Measurement is necessary, but it is not the destination

The World Economic Forum and Schwab Foundation paper, The Impact Economy: Turning Disclosure into Demand, was published on 8 October 2026. It notes that only 15% of Sustainable Development Goal targets with sufficient trend data are on track, even as businesses and institutions increasingly disclose social impacts.

The paper identifies an “impact paradox”: society has become better at describing social and environmental outcomes, yet those outcomes do not automatically influence purchasing, investment, regulation or other economic decisions. It proposes four pathways through which demand can become more concrete: regulatory, commercial, financial and collaborative.

That distinction is useful. A social enterprise may be able to prove that it improves livelihoods, reduces waste or increases access to a service. But evidence alone does not pay salaries or finance expansion. Sustainability depends on a credible route through which somebody—customers, public institutions, corporations, investors, donors or a combination of them—values the outcome enough to allocate resources to it.

Capital is also being asked to behave differently

On 4 November 2026, Social Finance announced two US$2.5 million commitments from its Impact First Fund: one to Enable Ventures Fund I, focused on the disability wealth gap, and another to the Fractal Agriculture Regenerative Management Fund, which provides an alternative form of capital to farmers.

These investments are in the United States, not Africa, so they should not be presented as funding opportunities for African founders. Their relevance is conceptual. Both examples show capital being structured around markets that conventional investors may under-serve, while still asking whether the underlying model can generate sustainable value.

For African entrepreneurs, the lesson is not that every social enterprise needs a specialist impact fund. It is that financing strategy should match the economics of the problem. A venture with strong recurring revenue may be suitable for equity or debt. A venture creating a public good may need grant or public-sector support. Some models may require blended finance, guarantees or patient capital. Treating every funding problem as a grant problem can be as limiting as treating every enterprise as a conventional venture-capital opportunity.

Nairobi is part of this wider conversation

On 5–6 November 2026, EdelGive/SIX is convening Wayfinder Nairobi, bringing together around 100 practitioners, researchers, funders and policymakers working on social innovation. The convening came ten years after the original SIX Wayfinder and was framed around what the field should do next.

There is a useful signal in the location. African social innovation should not be treated only as a pipeline of projects seeking external support. Cities such as Nairobi are increasingly places where practitioners, capital providers, researchers and institutions can shape the global conversation about what effective innovation looks like.

That creates an opportunity, but also a responsibility. Ecosystem visibility matters only if it improves the conditions under which serious enterprises can build: clearer procurement pathways, better evidence, appropriate capital, stronger institutions, access to markets, capable teams and honest accountability for results.

What this means for a social entrepreneur

For an entrepreneur building in Kenya or elsewhere in Africa, the practical question is not “How do I describe my impact more impressively?” A better set of questions is:

  • Who experiences the value? Be precise about the customer, beneficiary and wider stakeholder. They may not be the same person.
  • Who pays? Identify the party with both an incentive and the ability to fund the solution. If the beneficiary cannot pay, the business model must explain who can.
  • What evidence changes a decision? Measure what a buyer, investor, regulator or partner actually needs to know—not every indicator that could possibly be counted.
  • What is the financing need? Working capital, equipment, research, market entry and long-term infrastructure require different kinds of money.
  • What can scale without weakening the mission? Growth is useful only if the economics, quality and intended social or environmental value can survive expansion.

These questions also make fundraising more disciplined. Instead of beginning with “Where can I find a grant?”, a founder can begin with the enterprise itself: the problem, the customer, the operating model, the evidence, the unit economics, the barriers to scale and the type of capital appropriate to the next stage.

Impact should become part of the enterprise model

There is a temptation to treat impact measurement as a reporting layer added after the business model is already designed. That can lead to two separate stories: one for customers and investors, and another for donors or impact reports.

A stronger model connects them. If a clean-energy enterprise reduces household energy costs, those savings may be part of the customer value proposition. If a regenerative agriculture model improves resilience and productivity, the environmental outcome may also affect risk and long-term economics. If a skills enterprise improves employment outcomes, employers or public institutions may value better matching and retention.

This does not mean every social benefit can or should be monetised. Some outcomes are public goods. Some benefits appear over long periods. Some values should not be reduced to a price. The point is to understand which outcomes influence real decisions and where complementary public, philanthropic or collaborative financing is still required.

A practical discipline for African ecosystems

The same logic applies beyond individual ventures. Incubators, accelerators, universities, foundations and public agencies can help entrepreneurs move from “impact storytelling” towards stronger evidence and demand.

That means asking programmes to demonstrate what happens after training; helping founders test willingness to pay rather than only pitch decks; connecting enterprises to procurement and distribution; distinguishing grants from investment; and building reliable ways for public and private buyers to recognise verified social value.

It also means resisting inflated claims. A network connection is not a contract. An accelerator place is not investment. A pilot is not proven scale. A target is not an outcome. Responsible social entrepreneurship becomes more credible when it is willing to say what is known, what is still being tested and what evidence would change the next decision.

The next phase is about demand

The current direction of travel is encouraging. Impact measurement is becoming more sophisticated, impact-first capital is experimenting with overlooked markets, and ecosystem actors are meeting in places such as Nairobi to examine what comes next.

But the next phase of social entrepreneurship will not be won by better language alone. It will depend on whether measurable social and environmental value can influence how money, procurement, policy and partnerships actually move.

For African founders, that makes the challenge both harder and more practical: build something useful, know who values it, understand who can pay, prove what matters and choose capital that fits the enterprise rather than distorting it.
