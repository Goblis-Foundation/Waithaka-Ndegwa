On 4 November 2026, Aliko Dangote’s launch of the Namibia Tank Farm and Regional Pipeline Project at Walvis Bay brought a question back into focus for me: if the future of transport is supposed to be entirely electric, why is such substantial investment still flowing into liquid-fuel infrastructure?

According to The Namibian’s report from the launch, Dangote described a proposed US$2.6 billion investment and a 1,700-kilometre regional pipeline. Channels Television’s coverage describes proposed storage for at least 1.6 million barrels of petrol and diesel. These are announced investments and proposed capacities—not evidence that the entire terminal and cross-border network are already operating.

My answer is not that Dangote has proved electric vehicles will fail. An investor’s decision cannot settle that question. My reading is that the transition will be more uneven, more infrastructure-dependent and more complicated than replacing every fuel tank with a battery.

That is why I remain sceptical of an all-electric-only future, and why I believe sustainable biofuels deserve a much larger place in Africa’s industrial thinking.

Disclosure: I am a co-founder and project manager of Crostar Energy LTD. My interest in renewable oils and biofuels is practical and commercial, as well as environmental. This is my personal analysis; no commercial relationship with Dangote or Eni is implied.

From Lamu to Walvis Bay: what Dangote’s investments tell us

The Namibia announcement follows the groundbreaking of the Dangote East Africa Petroleum Refinery in Lamu on 30 September 2026, as recorded by Kenya’s Ministry of Defence. The Ministry of Roads and Transport describes a planned capacity of 700,000 barrels per day and the project’s connection to Lamu Port and the LAPSSET Corridor. Planned refinery capacity must not be confused with current output.

These are different projects: Lamu concerns refining and associated industrial development; Walvis Bay concerns petroleum storage and regional distribution. Neither announcement is, by itself, a biofuel project. Nor should they be presented as one continuous pipeline from Kenya to Namibia.

What interests me is the investment in the whole supply system: processing, ports, storage, transport and access to customers. Africa’s energy discussion cannot stop at the vehicle. Fuel must reach a farm, a construction site, a bus depot or an industrial customer reliably and at a workable cost.

There is a lesson here for renewable fuels. A promising crop or a laboratory sample is not an industry. Collection, testing, processing, storage, finance and dependable buyers must work together. But there is also a warning: new petroleum infrastructure can face demand and climate-policy risks. Its construction is not a guarantee of permanent demand, and suitability for future biofuel handling would require technical validation.

Turkana adds the missing link: a market can unlock infrastructure

The Business Daily report published on 3 November 2026, “Kenya revives Turkana oil pipeline for Dangote refinery,” adds an important dimension. It reports that Kenya is reviving plans to connect the Turkana oilfields to the planned Lamu refinery. Presidential economic adviser David Ndii argues that the refinery could make the pipeline financeable after earlier uncertainty about funding.

The report also makes clear that Turkana would be one of several crude sources for Lamu, not its entire supply. This remains a reported infrastructure plan—not confirmation that financing has closed, a pipeline is under construction or crude is already reaching the proposed refinery.

The lesson I draw is about the relationship between supply, a dependable buyer and infrastructure. A market at the other end can change whether investment appears viable. Renewable-fuel developers face a comparable commercial question: who will buy, against what specifications, at what price and for how long?

This does not make petroleum investment a climate solution. It suggests that biofuel ambitions must be supported by the same commercial discipline: reliable feedstock, qualified processing, credible customers and a route connecting them.

The evidence does not support declaring EVs finished

A serious case for biofuels should not depend on denying electric vehicles’ progress. The International Energy Agency’s Global EV Outlook 2026 reports that electric car sales exceeded 20 million in 2025—about one-quarter of new car sales. These figures include battery-electric cars and plug-in hybrids; they do not mean one-quarter of all cars already on the road are electric.

The IEA’s 30 July 2026 update raised its projected electric share of global new-car sales for 2026 to 29%, following a second-quarter rebound. That remains a forecast for the full year, not a completed-year result. The market is uneven, but it is not disappearing.

What looks bleak to me is the promise that one technology can meet every transport need on the same timetable. There is a difference between an EV that works well for a particular customer and a universal transition model that every household, business and country can afford.

Battery prices are falling. Affordability is a different question

Expensive batteries remain a legitimate concern, but the argument needs updating. BloombergNEF’s December 2025 survey put the average lithium-ion pack price across applications at US$108 per kilowatt-hour, down 8% from 2024. Battery-electric vehicle packs averaged US$99 per kilowatt-hour. It would be wrong to describe battery costs as universally rising.

Yet lower manufacturing prices do not automatically make ownership affordable. For illustration, multiplying a 60-kilowatt-hour pack by that US$99 survey average gives US$5,940. That is arithmetic using an industry average—not a retail replacement quote for Kenya. A customer’s actual bill depends on the vehicle, replacement pack, freight, taxes, labour and warranty arrangements.

The US Department of Energy’s maintenance guidance makes both sides clear: fully electric vehicles generally need less routine maintenance, but an out-of-warranty battery replacement can be a significant expense. That possibility should not become a claim that every owner will need a new battery after a few years.

My concern is who carries the financial risk. Before financing an imported used EV, I would want a credible battery-health assessment, written warranty terms, local diagnostic support and a clear repair pathway. The relevant calculation is not simply electricity versus petrol. It is purchase price, interest, energy, maintenance, downtime and resale value over the intended ownership period.

A lower running cost is valuable. It cannot, on its own, supply the deposit or reassure a lender.

A charging point is only one part of the system

Charging is not just about putting a socket beside a parking space. A usable network requires suitable connections, sufficient power at the right locations, reliable operation and a business model that keeps equipment maintained. For an operator, the question is practical: can the vehicle complete its route, recharge when needed and return to work without losing revenue?

The IEA’s 2026 assessment finds that electric trucks still cost two to three times as much to purchase as diesel trucks, even though their total cost of ownership is already competitive in China. It expects European total-ownership-cost parity around 2030. This is an argument for examining each market and duty cycle—not for claiming that heavy transport can never electrify.

Kenya also has reasons to pursue electrification. The government launched its National Electric Mobility Policy on 3 February 2026. A bus with predictable depot charging, an urban delivery vehicle and a motorcycle using a workable swapping network present different questions from a machine operating far from dependable charging.

Supply-chain resilience matters too. The IEA’s battery assessment documents concentrated manufacturing and recycling capacity. Battery recycling exists; it is not an unsolved impossibility. Nevertheless, importing vehicles without developing skills, repair capacity and responsible end-of-life arrangements would leave an important part of the transition unfinished.

A cleaner traffic jam is still a traffic jam

Ever-larger vehicles raise a separate transport problem. Changing the drivetrain does not remove congestion, road-space consumption or the need to move people efficiently. If improved technology encourages us to buy larger vehicles and travel more, some of its benefits can be eroded. That possibility is a reason to combine cleaner vehicles with better public transport and sensible vehicle sizing.

However, I would not make a blanket claim that a small petrol car always has a lower lifetime carbon footprint than a large electric SUV. Vehicle size, mileage, electricity supply and manufacturing all matter. The IEA’s 2026 lifecycle assessment estimates that a medium-sized battery-electric car has more than 55% lower lifetime greenhouse-gas emissions than a comparable petrol car under its stated-policy assumptions.

We should challenge wasteful transport systems without dismissing genuine emissions improvements. The same discipline must apply to biofuels: a cleaner fuel does not make an oversized, inefficient or unnecessary journey sustainable by definition.

Europe is creating demand for sustainable fuels—not abandoning EVs

The strongest evidence for biofuels is not a prediction that batteries will collapse. It is the existence of transport needs and policy-backed markets for lower-emission liquid fuels.

Under ReFuelEU Aviation, the minimum sustainable aviation fuel share began at 2% in 2025 and rises to 6% in 2030. SAF is a wider category than biofuel: eligible pathways also include synthetic fuels and recycled-carbon aviation fuels. A requirement for SAF is not a guaranteed market for every vegetable oil.

There is now evidence of actual supply. EASA’s 2026 report on 2025, clarified on 2 November 2026, records 1.1 million tonnes of SAF in suppliers’ reported deliveries, equivalent to 2.79% of their reported aviation fuel. It also reports an average SAF price of €1,925 per tonne against €640 for conventional jet fuel. The opportunity is real, but so is the cost challenge.

In shipping, FuelEU Maritime, fully applicable from January 2025, creates demand for lower greenhouse-gas-intensity energy. It is not a blanket biofuel mandate. Meanwhile, the revised Renewable Energy Directive gives EU countries a 2030 transport choice between 29% renewable energy and a 14.5% emissions-intensity reduction, alongside a combined 5.5% sub-target for advanced biofuels and renewable fuels of non-biological origin.

My conclusion is that Europe is pursuing a portfolio: electrification where it works, alongside sustainable fuels for other requirements. Africa should recognise the opportunity without pretending that European demand removes the need for competitive pricing and verified sustainability.

Biofuel is not a miracle crop—or raw oil poured into an engine

When I mention castor, jatropha, croton, sunflower or moringa, I mean potential feedstocks requiring assessment. I do not mean that every crop is automatically suitable, commercially attractive or eligible for every export market. Food uses, local growing conditions, oil characteristics, yields and the buyer’s specifications all change the answer.

Technical distinctions matter. Biodiesel production converts oils and fats into a different fuel; unprocessed vegetable oil and used cooking oil are not biodiesel and should not simply be poured into a diesel vehicle. Renewable diesel, often called HVO when produced through hydrotreating, is different again. Aviation fuel requires its own approved production and qualification pathways. An oil-extraction hub is not automatically a refinery producing certified jet fuel.

This is nevertheless where I see a practical opening: replacing a share of fossil fuel in suitable existing applications through correctly specified fuels and approved blends, rather than requiring every useful engine to be replaced immediately. The strongest opportunities are those where sustainable feedstock is available and electrification is less practical or slower to arrive.

The IPCC’s assessment identifies sustainable biofuels among the options for aviation, shipping and heavy-duty land transport, while emphasising the need for process improvements and cost reductions. It also identifies EVs powered by low-emission electricity as having the largest lifecycle decarbonisation potential for land transport. I see those findings as complementary, not contradictory.

Biofuels should be deployed where they provide demonstrable benefit, not used as a reason to delay electrification where it already works. Equally, a technology’s success in one segment should not close the door on a credible alternative in another.

The biofuel industry must face its own uncomfortable questions

Being non-edible does not make a crop free of competition with food. It can still require land, water, labour and finance. Clearing valuable ecosystems or undermining food production would weaken the very environmental case being made. The IPCC discusses these land, livelihood and food-security trade-offs. Lifecycle accounting must include cultivation, processing and transport—not simply the fact that a plant absorbed carbon.

Used cooking oil is attractive because it starts with an existing waste stream, but collection must be genuine and documented. The European Commission’s Union Database supports traceability and sustainability information across renewable-fuel supply chains. A green label cannot substitute for evidence of origin.

European interest has already reached Kenya. In May 2024, IFC and the Italian Climate Fund announced a US$210 million financing package for Eni’s Kenyan subsidiary to expand biofuel feedstock and processing activities. That demonstrates institutional interest; its farmer and production targets should not be reported as achieved outcomes.

The test for any such investment is what happens after the announcement. Are producers paid on time? Do harvests meet realistic assumptions? Can buyers verify origin and quality? Do processing costs leave a viable margin? Those are the questions I would ask of a multinational or a local startup alike.

Nor are biofuels necessarily cheap. EASA’s SAF price figures are a reminder that feedstock, processing, logistics and certification all have to be paid for. My case is not for subsidising any product called “green”; it is for building pathways that can demonstrate both useful emissions savings and a workable commercial model.

From climate declarations to African production

The Nairobi climate meetings that framed my original reflections took place in September 2023. They are no longer an upcoming event. The subsequent Addis Ababa Declaration from the second Africa Climate Summit, held on 8–10 September 2025, provides a more current continental reference.

It calls for a just transition grounded in national circumstances, renewables expansion, backbone energy infrastructure, green industrialisation and sustainable low-carbon fuels. It is not a resolution against electric vehicles or an endorsement of a particular company.

For me, the useful question is what we build from those principles. Can African producers earn fairly? Can we process more locally? Can customers trust the product? Can environmental claims survive scrutiny? Declarations matter, but they do not collect a litre of used oil, finance a processor or pay a farmer.

The purpose behind Crostar Energy

This is the purpose behind our work at Crostar Energy LTD: to develop a responsible, traceable renewable-oils and biofuel value chain connecting African producers and used-cooking-oil suppliers with processing and dependable markets. We aim to create local value, support livelihoods and contribute to lower-emission energy through suitable feedstocks and credible sustainability practices. That is why, despite the hurdles, I believe the work is worth pursuing.

My position: the future needs more than a plug

Dangote’s Namibia launch and the revived Turkana pipeline proposal are timely reminders to think in systems rather than slogans. They do not prove that petroleum wins forever, that EVs have failed, or that biofuels will automatically inherit existing infrastructure. They do challenge us to ask how the transition will actually reach customers.

I support electrification where it makes operational, financial and environmental sense. I also believe sustainable biofuels can play an indispensable role in parts of the transition—particularly where existing equipment, demanding duty cycles and hard-to-electrify transport create a need for lower-emission liquid fuels.

For Africa, the choice should not be reduced to importing batteries or importing petroleum. We should examine the scope to build our own renewable-oil and fuel industries, alongside clean electricity, better public transport and more efficient use of every vehicle.

The transition I believe in is one that people can afford, businesses can operate, farmers can benefit from, and environmental evidence can defend.

That is why I doubt an all-electric-only vision. The future I believe in gives sustainable biofuels a meaningful role—not as a slogan, but as part of a transport system that works.
