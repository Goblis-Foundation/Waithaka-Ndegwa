When Kenya announced a “visa-free” policy from January 2024, the idea was powerful: remove a traditional barrier and make the country easier to visit, trade with and experience. Two years later, the policy is more nuanced than the slogan.

Kenya replaced the ordinary visa process for many short-term visitors with an Electronic Travel Authorisation, or eTA. The current official system also includes important exemptions. Citizens of East African Community partner states listed by the Directorate of Immigration do not need an eTA for stays of up to 180 days, while nationals of a separate group of countries are exempt for up to 90 days under the 2025 immigration-rule amendments.

Visa-free does not mean paperwork-free

For travellers who still require an eTA, the authorisation must generally be approved before travel. Kenya’s official eTA site currently lists a standard eTA at US$30, a one-year multi-entry eTA at US$300, and a five-year multi-entry option for American nationals at US$185. The portal also makes clear that an eTA is permission to travel to Kenya; it is not itself a guarantee of admission at the border.

That is why I think “visa-free” should be understood as a policy direction rather than a literal description of every traveller’s process.

Kenya is competing for movement

Travel policy is economic infrastructure. A visitor deciding whether to attend a conference, inspect an investment, meet a supplier or take a holiday compares not only attractions but friction: flight connections, border processes, payment options, safety, digital services and the predictability of the rules.

For Kenya, easier mobility can support tourism, professional services, regional headquarters, events, agribusiness trade and innovation networks. Nairobi already functions as a regional meeting point. Reducing unnecessary friction strengthens that role.

The policy must also be easy to understand

A good travel system should make three things immediately clear: who is exempt, who needs authorisation, and what the traveller must do next. That information changes over time, which is why unofficial travel blogs and old social-media posts are a poor substitute for the government portal.

There is also a new 2026 compliance layer. Kenya’s eTA portal currently carries a Ministry of Health notice referring to Gazette Notice No. 11492 of 30 July 2026 and stating that inbound travellers are required to have valid travel health insurance under the stated framework. Travellers should therefore check the live official notice before departure rather than relying on guidance written in 2024.

Regional integration matters more than the slogan

The most important mobility story for me is still African integration. Citizens of EAC partner states already benefit from a deeper exemption. That is the direction I would like to see strengthened across the continent: easier legitimate movement for people doing business, studying, building partnerships, attending events and visiting one another.

Free movement will not solve the cost of African travel by itself. Air fares, limited route competition, taxes, airport charges and weak connections can still make a short trip across Africa more expensive than a much longer journey elsewhere. But border friction is one part of the system that governments can improve directly.

Digital convenience must remain accountable

An online authorisation system can be faster than a traditional visa process, but digital systems create their own risks: unofficial agents, fraudulent websites, unclear fees and people assuming that a successful online payment equals permission to enter.

I would advise any traveller to use the official Kenya eTA domain, check the exemption rules that apply to their passport, apply in good time if an eTA is required, and retain the approval and supporting documents for travel.

My view

Kenya was right to pursue a more open travel regime. The opportunity is bigger than tourism. Easier movement can support trade, investment, learning and regional cooperation. But the success of the policy should be judged by the traveller’s real experience, not by the simplicity of the headline.

The best system is one where a legitimate traveller can understand the rule quickly, complete the process safely, pay a transparent cost and arrive with confidence about what will happen next.
