I have long been interested in whether sugar beet could diversify Kenya’s sugar economy. The idea is attractive because Kenya continues to face a substantial sugar deficit, but the current evidence supports a more careful conclusion than simply calling sugar beet a national solution.

Sugar beet deserves serious agronomic and commercial trials in suitable Kenyan highlands. But it is not yet a proven national substitute for sugarcane, and current market data still records Kenya’s commercial sugar production as cane-based.

The deficit is real

The latest USDA Sugar Annual for Kenya forecasts 650,000 metric tonnes of sugar production in marketing year 2025/26 against consumption of about 1.25 million tonnes, with imports rising to cover the gap. The same report records beet-sugar production at zero.

That gap is exactly why alternative crops and processing models deserve attention. But a deficit by itself does not prove that a particular crop is economically suitable.

Beet has attractive characteristics

Sugar beet stores sucrose in its root and is a major global source of sugar, particularly in temperate climates. Compared with sugarcane, its shorter crop cycle can be attractive. The processing chain can also create useful co-products such as animal feed and soil-conditioning materials, while integrated processing can potentially support energy recovery.

Those features explain why the idea has repeatedly attracted interest in Kenyan highland areas.

Kenya needs evidence by agro-ecological zone

The key question is not whether beet grows somewhere in Kenya. It is whether specific varieties can produce enough recoverable sugar, reliably and profitably, under local soil, rainfall, temperature, pest and irrigation conditions.

Recent Kenyan research has continued to examine sugar beet in cooler highland environments, while also pointing to soil constraints that need to be understood. That is the right direction: test the crop as an agronomic system, not as a slogan.

A serious pilot should measure root yield, sugar content, water requirement, fertiliser use, pest pressure, harvesting cost, transport distance, storage losses and processing recovery. It should compare those economics with the farmer’s alternative crops—not only with sugarcane.

Processing is the harder half of the equation

Even if farmers can grow beet successfully, a sugar crop has little value without reliable processing capacity. Roots need to reach an appropriate factory within a workable logistics window. A processor needs enough predictable feedstock to justify capital-intensive equipment. Farmers need contracts or transparent pricing that prevent them from carrying all the production risk.

That means the opportunity is a value-chain question: seed, agronomy, aggregation, transport, factory technology, energy, by-products, working capital and market access.

Cane reform still matters

Kenya’s Sugar Act 2024 and the re-established Kenya Sugar Board place significant attention on revitalising the existing sugar industry. Improving cane productivity, factory efficiency, governance and research remains essential. Sugar beet should not become an excuse to avoid those reforms.

I see the stronger strategy as diversification. If beet can prove itself in selected highland zones, it could complement cane, expand farmer choice and create processing opportunities. If trials show the economics do not work, that is also valuable knowledge.

Start with a commercial pilot, not a national promise

For an agribusiness entrepreneur, this is the kind of idea that should move through stages:

  • agronomic trials with research partners;
  • small demonstration plots across more than one season;
  • independent sugar-content and yield measurement;
  • a processing feasibility study;
  • offtake and farmer-contract design;
  • then a bounded commercial pilot.

The most dangerous step would be recruiting thousands of farmers before processing economics and market arrangements are proven.

My view

I think sugar beet is worth investigating in Kenya. It is not “the solution” today. It is a potentially useful diversification pathway that should earn its place through evidence.

That is how I increasingly think about agribusiness innovation: not by asking whether an idea sounds promising, but whether the whole system—from soil to factory to buyer—can work repeatedly and profitably.
