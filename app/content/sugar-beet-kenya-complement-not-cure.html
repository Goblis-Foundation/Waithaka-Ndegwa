I have long been interested in whether sugar beet could diversify Kenya’s sugar economy. The idea is attractive because Kenya continues to face a substantial sugar deficit, but the current evidence supports a more careful conclusion than simply calling sugar beet a national solution.

Sugar beet deserves serious agronomic and commercial trials in suitable Kenyan highlands. But it is not yet a proven national substitute for sugarcane, and current market data still records Kenya’s commercial sugar production as cane-based.

The deficit is real

The latest USDA Sugar Annual for Kenya forecasts 650,000 metric tonnes of sugar production in marketing year 2025/26 against consumption of about 1.25 million tonnes, with imports rising to cover the gap. The same report records beet-sugar production at zero.

That gap is exactly why alternative crops and processing models deserve attention. But a deficit by itself does not prove that a particular crop is economically suitable—and Kenya's trade regime has historically managed that deficit through controlled regional market access rather than simply opening the border to any volume of sugar.

COMESA changed how Kenya managed the deficit

Kenya has been part of the COMESA Free Trade Area, under which qualifying goods from participating Member States ordinarily receive preferential market access. Sugar, however, was treated differently for more than two decades because Kenya argued that its domestic industry was not yet competitive enough to absorb unrestricted regional competition.

Beginning in 2002, Kenya operated under a COMESA sugar safeguard. The safeguard placed a quantitative limit on preferential imports of mill white and brown sugar while Kenya was expected to restructure its domestic industry. COMESA repeatedly extended the arrangement, but the extension granted from 1 December 2023 to 30 November 2025 was explicitly described as the final extension and was not to be renewed.

The quota was not simply an arbitrary cap. COMESA required Kenya to estimate its domestic sugar deficit and allocate import opportunities among eligible sugar-producing Member States. For the later safeguard period, COMESA adopted a minimum allocation base of 282,227 metric tonnes or Kenya's forecast deficit, whichever was higher. Member States that supplied Kenya also had quota-allocation and utilisation rules, including procedures for reallocating unutilised quantities.

COMESA also made an important policy point: if Kenya faced a shortage beyond the allocated quantities, it was expected to notify COMESA Member States and give regional suppliers the opportunity to fill that shortage before resorting to non-COMESA exporters. In other words, the safeguard protected Kenya's industry, but it was still designed to preserve regional trade rather than create permanent isolation.

The safeguard has expired, but imports are still regulated

The COMESA safeguard expired on 30 November 2025. I have not found a subsequent COMESA decision renewing it; the 2023 Council decision expressly said the final extension would not be renewed. That means the old COMESA quota safeguard should not be described as Kenya's current permanent trade regime.

Kenya's Sugar Act 2024 nevertheless keeps domestic import discipline. It requires sugar imports to be tied to an identified deficit and a specific tonnage, requires importers to obtain licences and pre-import approvals, and subjects those controls to Kenya's regional and international trade obligations.

This distinction matters. A country can simultaneously have a structural production deficit, protect domestic producers from disruptive import surges, and still be obliged to honour regional market-access commitments. The policy question is therefore not simply whether Kenya imports sugar, but how much, from where, under what rules, and whether domestic reforms are making the industry more competitive.

Beet has attractive characteristics

Sugar beet stores sucrose in its root and is a major global source of sugar, particularly in temperate climates. Compared with sugarcane, its shorter crop cycle can be attractive. The processing chain can also create useful co-products such as animal feed and soil-conditioning materials, while integrated processing can potentially support energy recovery.

Those features explain why the idea has repeatedly attracted interest in Kenyan highland areas.

Kenya needs evidence by agro-ecological zone

The key question is not whether beet grows somewhere in Kenya. It is whether specific varieties can produce enough recoverable sugar, reliably and profitably, under local soil, rainfall, temperature, pest and irrigation conditions.

Recent Kenyan research has continued to examine sugar beet in cooler highland environments, while also pointing to soil constraints that need to be understood. That is the right direction: test the crop as an agronomic system, not as a slogan.

A serious pilot should measure root yield, sugar content, water requirement, fertiliser use, pest pressure, harvesting cost, transport distance, storage losses and processing recovery. It should compare those economics with the farmer’s alternative crops—not only with sugarcane.

Processing is the harder half of the equation

Even if farmers can grow beet successfully, a sugar crop has little value without reliable processing capacity. Roots need to reach an appropriate factory within a workable logistics window. A processor needs enough predictable feedstock to justify capital-intensive equipment. Farmers need contracts or transparent pricing that prevent them from carrying all the production risk.

That means the opportunity is a value-chain question: seed, agronomy, aggregation, transport, factory technology, energy, by-products, working capital and market access.

Cane reform still matters

Kenya's Sugar Act 2024 and the re-established Kenya Sugar Board place significant attention on revitalising the existing sugar industry. Improving cane productivity, factory efficiency, governance and research remains essential. The end of the COMESA safeguard also increases the importance of competitiveness because a temporary regional protection mechanism cannot substitute indefinitely for lower production costs, better recovery rates and more efficient mills.

Recent government policy has also moved beyond table sugar alone. In 2026, the Ministry of Agriculture publicly backed greater use of sugarcane for ethanol and electricity generation, arguing that value addition can improve mill economics and reduce dependence on imported energy. That reinforces the case for thinking about the sugar sector as an integrated industrial system rather than a single commodity.

Sugar beet should therefore not become an excuse to avoid cane-sector reform. It should be assessed as an additional production and processing pathway that must compete on evidence.

I see the stronger strategy as diversification. If beet can prove itself in selected highland zones, it could complement cane, expand farmer choice and create processing opportunities. If trials show the economics do not work, that is also valuable knowledge.

Start with a commercial pilot, not a national promise

For an agribusiness entrepreneur, this is the kind of idea that should move through stages:

  • agronomic trials with research partners;
  • small demonstration plots across more than one season;
  • independent sugar-content and yield measurement;
  • a processing feasibility study;
  • offtake and farmer-contract design;
  • then a bounded commercial pilot.

The most dangerous step would be recruiting thousands of farmers before processing economics and market arrangements are proven.

My view

I think sugar beet is worth investigating in Kenya. It is not “the solution” today. It is a potentially useful diversification pathway that should earn its place through evidence.

The COMESA experience strengthens that conclusion. Kenya had more than two decades of exceptional protection to restructure its sugar industry, yet the country still has to manage the tension between domestic production, consumer prices, regional trade obligations and periodic import requirements. A new crop will not remove those institutional and industrial questions.

If sugar beet can produce competitive recoverable sugar in selected Kenyan highlands, feed a viable processing system and reduce the cost of meeting domestic demand, it deserves a place in the sector. If it cannot, import quotas or trade safeguards will not make the underlying economics work.

That is how I increasingly think about agribusiness innovation: not by asking whether an idea sounds promising, but whether the whole system—from soil to factory to buyer—can work repeatedly and profitably.
