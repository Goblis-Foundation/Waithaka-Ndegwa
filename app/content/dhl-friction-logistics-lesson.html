One of the business stories I keep returning to began with a surprisingly small problem. In 1969, Adrian Dalsey, Larry Hillblom and Robert Lynn founded DHL in San Francisco. Their early insight was not “build a global logistics giant.” It was much more practical: shipping paperwork could be flown ahead of cargo so customs processing could begin before the goods arrived.

That idea reduced waiting. It attacked friction before it attacked scale.

Infrastructure is also process

When we discuss infrastructure in Africa, we naturally think about roads, ports, railways, airports, power and fibre. Those assets are essential. But the DHL story is a reminder that physical infrastructure can be underused when the process around it is slow.

A port can have cranes and berths and still lose competitiveness to documentation delays. A farmer can produce a high-quality crop and still lose value because certification, cold-chain coordination or customs clearance fails. A small manufacturer can have a buyer abroad and still struggle because delivery is unpredictable.

Infrastructure therefore includes the systems that make movement reliable: information, documentation, standards, payments, scheduling and accountability.

DHL grew by solving the next constraint

DHL’s expansion from document courier to parcels, freight, supply chain and e-commerce logistics reveals a useful pattern. The company did not stay attached to the exact product it launched with. It stayed attached to the underlying customer problem: moving goods and information across distance with more certainty.

That is an important distinction for entrepreneurs. A company’s first product is not always its permanent identity. The deeper question is what problem the enterprise has earned the right to keep solving.

The African relevance is becoming more visible

DHL Group now reports operations in more than 220 countries and territories, approximately 584,000 employees and around €83 billion in 2025 revenue. More importantly for this discussion, the Group announced a planned investment of more than €300 million in Sub-Saharan Africa across DHL Express, Global Forwarding and Supply Chain.

The stated focus includes e-commerce, energy, perishables and life sciences and healthcare. Those are sectors where logistics quality can determine whether an African enterprise reaches a larger market.

For agribusiness, cold chain and predictable export handling can protect product value. For energy and infrastructure projects, complex equipment has to arrive safely and on time. For e-commerce, customers judge the digital transaction partly by what happens physically after they click “buy”.

AfCFTA needs logistics that works in practice

The African Continental Free Trade Area creates an extraordinary policy framework, but tariffs are only one layer of trade. If a shipment takes too long, documentation is inconsistent, border processes are unpredictable or reverse logistics is impossible, a theoretical market remains difficult to serve.

That is why I see logistics as a central enterprise opportunity. Not every solution requires a fleet of aircraft or a billion-dollar hub. Some of the most valuable African logistics businesses may begin by coordinating fragmented services, digitising documents, aggregating demand, improving tracking, creating trusted fulfilment, or solving the last kilometre for a specific sector.

The founder lesson: find the delay

The DHL origin story offers a simple exercise for any entrepreneur: identify where time is being wasted.

  • What does a customer wait for before they can continue?
  • Which document is processed only after it could have been processed?
  • Where is information repeated manually?
  • Which approval arrives too late?
  • Which physical asset sits idle because another part of the process is not ready?

Those delays are often business opportunities because reducing them creates measurable value.

Scale should follow reliability

DHL’s present scale can make the company feel impossible to learn from. I think the useful part is the opposite. Its beginning was narrow. The founders solved a specific operational problem, built a service around it, and expanded as the network and customer need grew.

For African enterprises, that is a healthier model than starting with the ambition to be “the biggest” before the service is reliable. Solve one expensive friction point. Measure the improvement. Build trust. Then extend the system.

That is how infrastructure becomes enterprise—and how a small process innovation can eventually reshape an industry.
