Tong Wenhong’s rise inside Alibaba is often told as a simple story about perseverance: joining at the front desk, taking on more responsibility, and eventually becoming one of the company’s partners. The story is worth examining, but it deserves a more careful reading than the motivational versions that circulate online.

Alibaba’s current Partnership disclosure lists Judy Wenhong Tong as having joined Alibaba Group in 2000 and as a current Partner of the Alibaba Partnership. It also shows that Jane Fang Jiang—not Tong—is the Group’s current Chief People Officer. That distinction matters because old profiles can easily become stale when repeated without checking.

The career story is more useful than the legend

Earlier accounts describe Tong beginning in an entry-level administrative role and later moving through customer service, administration, human resources and senior leadership. By 2014 she was among Alibaba’s original group of partners when the company listed in New York.

A popular version of the story also says Jack Ma promised her a particular equity stake and urged her to remain until an IPO. I have not found a current Alibaba filing that substantiates the specific 0.2% anecdote, so I would not present that number as established fact. The verified point is already significant enough: a person who joined near the bottom of a young organisation later became part of its partnership structure.

Long-term value is built before it is rewarded

What interests me as an entrepreneur is not the fantasy that loyalty automatically produces wealth. It does not. Staying in the wrong organisation for a long time can be costly. A business can also exploit loyalty if it does not create fair pathways for people to grow.

The more useful lesson is that organisations create extraordinary value when people are allowed to expand beyond the job description they were first hired to perform.

Early-stage enterprises often recruit for immediate tasks: administration, sales, operations, customer support. But a founder should also ask who is learning the system, who sees problems others miss, who can be trusted with a broader responsibility, and who is developing the judgement needed for leadership.

Ownership changes how people think

I believe social enterprises in particular should think seriously about how value is shared. That does not mean every employee must receive equity, and ownership structures must fit the law, capital model and stage of the enterprise. But people who help build long-term value should have credible ways to participate in the upside—through equity, profit-sharing, performance rewards, professional growth or other transparent mechanisms.

Too many enterprises talk about employees as “family” while keeping all meaningful economic participation at the top. That language can become hollow. If people are expected to think like owners, there should be a serious conversation about what ownership, agency and reward actually mean.

Titles should follow capability, not the other way around

Tong’s career is also a reminder that capability can emerge from unexpected places. The receptionist may understand customers, suppliers and internal bottlenecks better than someone recruited directly into a senior role. The field officer may know why a programme is failing before headquarters sees it. The junior technician may understand the user problem before the strategy team does.

Founders sometimes make the mistake of overvaluing credentials at entry and undervaluing demonstrated learning after entry. A growing organisation needs both: people with specialist expertise and people whose capability becomes visible through the work.

Verify the inspiring story

There is a second lesson here for how we consume business stories. Motivational posts often compress a complicated career into a few dramatic numbers and quotes. That may make a better social-media story, but it can weaken the real lesson.

The current Alibaba record is more modest and more credible: Tong joined in 2000 and remains a Partner of the Alibaba Partnership in 2026. Jane Jiang has been Chief People Officer since 2023. Those facts are enough to support a serious discussion about internal mobility, institutional memory and the value of developing people over time.

What I take from the story

When I think about the institutions and enterprises I want to help build, the question is not simply how to recruit talented people. It is how to create an environment in which people can become more capable than the role they first entered.

A strong organisation should be able to say: we noticed contribution, we trusted people with bigger problems, we created pathways to leadership, and when value was created, we found fair ways to recognise it.
