The Christmas Truce of 1914 has always struck me as one of history’s most unsettling reminders that institutions can put people into conflict without completely erasing their humanity. The familiar story includes carols across trenches, soldiers stepping into no-man’s-land, exchanging small gifts and, in some places, playing football. The core story is true, but the history is more precise than the popular legend.

The National Army Museum records that unofficial ceasefires took place along parts of the Western Front during Christmas 1914. They were not one centrally organised truce, and fighting continued elsewhere. Where truces occurred, British and German soldiers sang, exchanged food, tobacco and small items, met in no-man’s-land and used the pause to retrieve and bury fallen comrades. Football was played in some places, but there was no single great match involving the whole front.

The lesson is not that conflict disappeared

Hostilities resumed. Senior commands discouraged fraternisation, and there was no major repeat on the same scale during the rest of the war. That matters because the meaning of the Christmas Truce should not be romanticised into a claim that goodwill alone can dissolve structural conflict.

What interests me is something narrower and, in many ways, more powerful: people who had been trained to see one another as enemies still recognised each other as human when a small opening appeared.

Leadership often begins before authority changes

In enterprise and public life, we often talk about leadership as if it begins with title, command or policy. The truce shows another dimension. Some front-line soldiers made small choices within a brutal system: not to fire for a moment, to answer a greeting, to exchange food, to recover a body with dignity. Junior officers in some places tolerated those actions even when senior leadership opposed fraternisation.

I do not think that means rules or command structures are unimportant. Institutions exist for reasons. But leadership also includes judgement: knowing when a human consideration deserves attention even inside a rigid system.

Trust can begin with very small signals

Modern entrepreneurship, partnerships and community work are far removed from a battlefield, but the mechanics of distrust are familiar. People enter negotiations carrying assumptions about one another. Organisations inherit rivalries. Communities sometimes expect outsiders to arrive with ready-made answers. Founders can become so focused on competition that every other player looks like a threat.

Trust rarely begins with a grand agreement. It begins with a small credible signal: sharing information accurately, honouring a commitment, acknowledging uncertainty, listening before proposing, or simply treating the person on the other side as someone with legitimate interests.

That is one reason I place so much weight on verifiable information and transparent follow-through in the work I care about. Trust grows when words and actions agree.

Humanity is not weakness

There is a persistent idea that empathy makes leaders soft. I see the opposite. A leader who can recognise another person’s humanity without becoming confused about responsibilities is often better equipped to negotiate, resolve disputes and build durable institutions.

The soldiers of 1914 did not stop being soldiers. The war did not end. Yet, briefly, men who had every institutional reason to remain distant crossed a psychological boundary before they crossed the physical space between trenches.

For me, the Christmas Truce is therefore not a sentimental story about peace magically appearing. It is a reminder that even powerful systems do not completely determine how individuals behave inside them.

What I take from it today

Whether we are building enterprises, institutions, partnerships or communities, we still make choices about how we see the people around us. We can reduce them to roles—competitor, regulator, customer, employee, donor, beneficiary—or we can remember that every system ultimately depends on human beings making decisions.

Good leadership requires structure, accountability and sometimes firm disagreement. But it should also preserve dignity. If people in the trenches of the First World War could find moments of shared humanity under conditions almost impossible to imagine, then our own disagreements should rarely require us to abandon respect.
