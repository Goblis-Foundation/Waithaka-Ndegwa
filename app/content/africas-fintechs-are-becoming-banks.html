Africa’s fintech story is changing. The first chapter was about moving money. The next one is increasingly about holding money, lending it, regulating it and building the balance-sheet economics of a bank.

That shift is becoming much easier to see in 2026. Flutterwave obtained a Nigerian microfinance banking licence in April. Paystack moved into licensed banking through its acquisition of Ladder Microfinance Bank earlier in the year. Moniepoint had already deepened its banking position through a National Microfinance Bank licence. Tyme has shown that a digital-native bank can reach scale and profitability. And MTN has openly discussed seeking banking licences in African markets so it can take deposits and deepen lending.

These are not identical business models, and the dates and regulatory paths differ. But they point in the same direction: the most important African fintech companies no longer want to sit only on top of the banking system. They increasingly want to own more of the financial relationship itself.

Payments were the entry point

African fintech grew first by solving obvious frictions in payments. Businesses needed easier collections. Consumers needed cheaper and faster transfers. Developers needed APIs. Merchants needed acceptance tools. Cross-border transactions were expensive and fragmented. Banks were often slow to serve small businesses and younger customers.

That created an extraordinary wave of innovation. Payments companies could grow rapidly without carrying all the obligations of a bank balance sheet. They could focus on customer experience, software, distribution and transaction infrastructure while regulated banks performed important functions underneath: safeguarding deposits, settlement, account infrastructure and access to the formal banking system.

But the economics of payments alone can be thin. A company may process billions of dollars while capturing only a small fee on each transaction. The deeper economics sit in deposits, lending, treasury, cash management and the broader financial relationship.

This is why the current shift matters. The question is no longer only who processes the transaction. It is who holds the account, who understands the cash flow, who can lend responsibly against that cash flow, and who becomes the primary financial institution for the customer.

Flutterwave’s licence makes the transition explicit

Flutterwave is one of the clearest examples. In April 2026, the company announced that it had secured a Nigerian microfinance banking licence. That matters because Flutterwave was built as payments infrastructure. It connected merchants, consumers and businesses across African markets without itself being a conventional deposit-taking bank.

The licence gives the company a different strategic position. Instead of depending entirely on partner banks for account infrastructure and settlement, it can move closer to the regulated core of banking in Nigeria.

That creates several possibilities. A merchant that already uses Flutterwave for collections could eventually use the same ecosystem for deposits, cash management, working capital or other banking services allowed under the licence. The company can capture more of the value chain while reducing dependence on sponsor-bank arrangements for some services.

It also changes the importance of data. Payments companies see transaction histories, seasonality, merchant cash flows and customer behaviour. If used responsibly and under appropriate regulation, those signals can improve underwriting for businesses that traditional banks often struggle to assess.

That is particularly important for African SMEs. Many viable businesses are rejected by traditional lenders because they lack conventional collateral, formal audited histories or the paperwork banks are accustomed to reviewing. A financial institution that already sees the business’s operating activity may be able to assess risk differently.

Paystack is moving in the same direction

Paystack’s move into banking reinforces the point. In January 2026, the company acquired Ladder Microfinance Bank in Nigeria. Paystack had built its reputation as payments infrastructure for African businesses. The acquisition gives it a regulated vehicle through which it can participate more directly in banking.

This does not mean every payment company will become a full commercial bank. Regulatory structures vary, and microfinance banking licences have their own limits. But it does show that payments businesses increasingly see value in owning regulated banking capacity rather than remaining indefinitely dependent on external banks.

The strategy is logical. Payments provide distribution. Banking provides financial depth.

Once a company has acquired a merchant, the next opportunity is not necessarily another payment product. It may be a business account, working capital, payroll, cards, savings, foreign-exchange services or other financial tools that increase the value of the customer relationship.

Moniepoint shows what deeper financial integration can look like

Moniepoint offers another version of the same transition. It began as financial infrastructure and became increasingly embedded in the daily operations of Nigerian businesses. Its ecosystem now includes payments, merchant services, accounts, credit and business-management tools.

Moniepoint’s microfinance bank was upgraded to a national licence before 2026, which matters because it gave the group the regulatory foundation to expand banking services across Nigeria.

The important lesson is not the licence by itself. It is what happens when a company combines regulatory authority with a huge merchant network.

A merchant terminal can be a customer-acquisition channel. But the larger opportunity is to become the merchant’s financial operating system.

Who processes the sales?

Who holds the account?

Who provides the working capital?

Who manages payroll?

Who issues the cards?

Who provides the business tools?

The institution that answers several of those questions creates a much stronger relationship than a company that earns one transaction fee.

Tyme proves that digital-native banking can work

Tyme is slightly different from Flutterwave, Paystack and Moniepoint because TymeBank was built as a bank from the beginning of its public operations. It is therefore not an example of a fintech later obtaining a banking licence.

It is something equally important: proof that a digital-native banking model can scale in Africa.

TymeBank launched publicly in South Africa in 2019 and recorded its first profitable month in December 2023. By 2024 it had millions of customers, billions of rand in deposits and a growing lending book. In December 2024, parent group Tyme raised US$250 million at a US$1.5 billion valuation in a round led by Nubank.

The Nubank connection is particularly interesting. Nubank proved in Latin America that a technology-led bank could challenge entrenched financial institutions at enormous scale. Its investment in Tyme suggests that the digital-banking opportunity in emerging markets is increasingly being seen as globally transferable rather than geographically isolated.

Tyme also demonstrates that digital banking does not necessarily mean digital-only distribution. Its model combines technology with physical retail partnerships and assisted onboarding. That matters in African markets where cash, agents and physical touchpoints remain important.

Why deposits matter more than app downloads

Customer numbers attract attention. Deposits matter more.

A bank that can attract stable deposits has access to a lower-cost source of funding than one that depends heavily on wholesale borrowing or repeated external capital raises. Those deposits can support lending, subject to capital, liquidity and regulatory requirements.

That is where the economic model changes dramatically.

A payments company earns when money moves.

A bank can earn from payments, deposits, lending, treasury, cash management, cards and other financial services over a much longer customer lifecycle.

This is one reason the next phase of African fintech may be less visible but more economically important than the first. The first wave digitised transactions. The second wave has to deepen financial intermediation.

Africa does not only need better ways to move money. It needs better ways to allocate capital.

Can a farmer finance equipment?

Can a small manufacturer buy machinery?

Can a trader obtain working capital based on genuine cash flow?

Can a growing startup access debt without surrendering excessive equity?

Can households build savings securely?

Those questions are much closer to banking than to payments.

Credit could become the real prize

The African credit gap remains enormous, particularly for SMEs. Traditional lending models often depend on collateral, formal records and long-established banking relationships. Many small businesses do not fit that profile even when their underlying businesses are viable.

Fintech platforms may have an advantage because they can observe transaction behaviour directly. They may know how much a merchant sells, how frequently revenue arrives, how volatile the cash flow is and whether the business is growing.

That does not eliminate credit risk. It simply creates better information.

Used carefully, transaction data can support more precise underwriting. Used recklessly, it can accelerate bad lending just as quickly as good lending.

This is where fintech culture will collide with banking reality.

Growth at all costs is dangerous in lending. A payments company can survive high customer acquisition costs for years if investors continue financing expansion. A lender eventually has to collect the money it advances.

The winners will need to combine fintech speed with banking discipline.

Becoming a bank means inheriting banking risks

A banking licence is not simply a better fintech licence.

It comes with capital requirements, liquidity management, provisioning, governance, anti-money-laundering obligations, consumer protection, cybersecurity responsibilities and regulatory reporting.

It also creates a different social obligation. Depositors are trusting the institution with their money.

A beautiful application cannot compensate for weak underwriting.

Rapid customer growth cannot repair poor liquidity management.

Artificial intelligence cannot make reckless lending sustainable.

Venture capital cannot indefinitely subsidise a structurally unprofitable balance sheet.

This is why the transition from payments into banking should not be romanticised. It is a move into a harder business.

The technology may be new. The fundamental risks of banking are not.

The telcos may be the most dangerous competitors

There is another group in this race that should not be underestimated: telecommunications companies.

African telcos already possess something most fintechs spend heavily to acquire—distribution.

Mobile-money platforms have tens of millions of active users. They already handle everyday payments, merchant transactions, bills, remittances and increasingly savings and credit partnerships.

MTN made this strategic direction unusually clear in 2026 by discussing the possibility of seeking banking licences in African markets. The logic is obvious. If a telco can take deposits directly and extend more lending from its own balance sheet, it moves from being a mobile-money operator into something much closer to a bank.

That would create a remarkable competitive landscape.

A fintech may have superior software.

A bank may have the licence, capital base and regulatory experience.

A telco may have the largest customer network and the most frequent customer interaction.

What happens when all three start offering increasingly similar products?

Traditional banks still have enormous advantages

It would be a mistake to assume that fintech companies will simply replace incumbent banks.

Traditional African banks still possess advantages that are extremely difficult to reproduce.

They have capital.

They have regulatory history.

They have treasury capability.

They have corporate customers.

They have credit experience.

They have deposit bases.

They have foreign-exchange infrastructure.

And many now have strong digital channels of their own.

The strongest incumbent bank may therefore not fight fintech from the outside. It may become increasingly fintech-like itself.

Modernise the core.

Open APIs.

Reduce branch dependence.

Use better data.

Embed financial services into customer workflows.

Acquire specialist fintech capabilities where that makes sense.

Over time, the distinction between a bank and a fintech may matter less than it does today.

The real distinction will be between institutions that can combine technology, trust, distribution and financial discipline—and those that cannot.

Africa may produce a different kind of bank

This is where the opportunity becomes genuinely interesting.

Africa does not need to copy the historical banking architecture of Europe or North America.

The continent can build financial institutions adapted to its own realities.

A bank that is digital but understands cash.

A bank that uses merchants, agents and retailers rather than relying entirely on expensive branches.

A bank that understands informal and small businesses because it sees their transactions every day.

A bank that can serve a trader, a farmer, a software company and a multinational through different interfaces built on the same infrastructure.

Tyme begins with digital banking and retail distribution.

Moniepoint begins with merchants and business infrastructure.

Flutterwave begins with payments and APIs.

Paystack begins with merchant payments.

MTN begins with telecommunications and mobile money.

Those are not traditional banking starting points.

That may be precisely why they matter.

The next battle is for the financial relationship

Africa’s first fintech battle was for the transaction.

Who could move money faster, more cheaply and more conveniently?

The next battle is for the financial relationship.

Who holds the deposit?

Who understands the cash flow?

Who provides the credit?

Who manages the merchant?

Who owns the customer interface?

Who controls enough of the infrastructure to deliver services efficiently?

And who can do all of that while maintaining the trust and discipline required of a regulated financial institution?

That is a much bigger contest.

So when Flutterwave obtains a banking licence, when Paystack buys a microfinance bank, when Moniepoint deepens its banking structure, when Tyme proves that digital banking can scale, and when MTN starts openly discussing deposit-taking licences, these should not be treated as isolated announcements.

They are pieces of the same structural change.

Africa’s fintech industry is moving from the edges of banking towards its core.

The winners may still call themselves fintechs.

They may call themselves banks.

They may be telecommunications companies.

The label will matter less than the economics.

The most valuable position in African financial services will probably not belong simply to the company with the best app. It will belong to the institution that earns the customer’s trust, holds the relationship, understands the underlying economic activity, allocates capital responsibly and can remain profitable while doing it at enormous scale.

That is why the more useful question is no longer whether African fintechs will continue disrupting banks.

It is which of today’s fintechs, telcos and incumbents will become Africa’s next generation of banks.
